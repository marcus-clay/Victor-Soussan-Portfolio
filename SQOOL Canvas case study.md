# **Case study 3000 words**

## **Canvas – Exploring real-time collaboration in education**

**A speculative whiteboard app for classrooms and teachers**

*(2021, UNOWHY)*

---

### **Introduction**

In early 2021, as the dust was beginning to settle after the first waves of the COVID pandemic, we started asking ourselves a difficult question: *what does collaboration look like in classrooms now?*

The idea for Canvas didn’t come from market benchmarking or feature parity. It emerged from the gaps — the emotional, cognitive, and infrastructural cracks we’d witnessed during months of remote teaching. A new tool wasn’t the answer. A new mindset was.

Canvas was our speculative attempt at rethinking the dynamic between teachers and students — an attempt to translate modern digital collaboration into pedagogical rhythms. Think FigJam, Miro or Excalidraw — but built for the classroom, not for startups. A whiteboard, yes, but designed with an acute understanding of what teachers do when they prepare a lesson, run a session, manage attention, and collect feedback.

---

### **Starting from lived reality**

Before building anything, we spent several weeks interviewing teachers, observing classes, running desk research and reading pedagogical reviews like the [Teluq article on digital distance mediation](https://revue-mediations.teluq.ca/index.php/Distances/article/view/168/128). We didn’t go in with a vision to “reinvent collaboration.” We wanted to understand what had failed during remote learning, and why.

Some key insights shaped the whole trajectory of the project:

* **Teachers don’t co-create** in real-time like designers do. Collaboration in classrooms is *structured*, often *asynchronous*, and bounded by *pedagogical sequencing*.

* Most teachers still relied on email or ENT platforms to distribute and collect documents, or WhatsApp groups for class reminders. The tools weren’t connected. Neither were the workflows.

* Many educators were skeptical of real-time interaction with students, especially when it risked opening unstructured moments in the session that they couldn’t moderate.

* Students loved visual tools, tactile interaction on tablets, and being able to contribute with minimal friction — if they understood the rules.

These insights weren’t blockers. They became our design constraints.

---

### **The initial vision: not a Miro clone**

We scoped the Canvas project around a few clear premises:

* **Web-based, multi-platform**, accessible from the tablet, PC, and interactive whiteboard

* **No login barrier** — access via session codes or classroom authentication

* **Role-specific interfaces** — teachers control the canvas; students contribute when invited

* **Timeline-based** — teachers can sequence phases: prepare \> open for students \> freeze \> review

* **Templated activities** — not just a blank canvas: Venn diagrams, timelines, post-its, image zones…

It was never about making a digital whiteboard. It was about choreographing participation.

---

### **Low-fidelity, high-fidelity, conceptual fidelity**

We started with paper prototypes and animations. The goal was to show, not tell — to embody the rhythm of a session.

I built the first interactive prototypes using simple animated flows to illustrate:

* How a teacher starts a new canvas

* How the interface adapts when projecting on a whiteboard

* How students contribute from tablets

* How feedback or voting could be collected in real time

We iterated fast. Some ideas were discarded: free-form drawing created chaos. Voice feedback was too slow. Real-time co-ownership of zones led to confusion.

Instead, we focused on the *sequencing* logic: one person drives, others contribute, then we freeze. It’s a jazz band, not a jam session.

---

### **A story of divergence: Canvas vs Gestion de classe**

At this point, we were also conducting work on the *Gestion de classe* modernization project. This was about helping teachers manage the digital classroom: lock screens, control app usage, guide attention.

Canvas and Gestion de classe started from the same root need — how do we enable teachers in hybrid or digital classrooms — but took diverging paths.

* *Gestion de classe* was **supervised orchestration**.

* *Canvas* was **exploratory collaboration**.

Both responded to the post-COVID digital maturity of classrooms. But while Gestion de classe evolved into the production app *SQOOL Classe*, Canvas remained a demonstrator — a speculative, prototyped concept without a delivery roadmap.

---

### **Critical learnings**

Even if Canvas never shipped, the project left a deep mark. It helped us push design conversations further inside the company, and frame useful provocations in our internal strategy decks. Here’s what we learned:

#### **1\. Product-market timing is everything**

In 2021, teachers weren’t ready. The hardware existed, but the mindset didn’t. We would have built something *technically feasible* but *culturally unused*.

#### **2\. Collaboration in education is not real-time by default**

Unlike Figma or Miro users, teachers value structured, time-boxed, hierarchical roles. Tools need to reflect this pedagogical structure.

#### **3\. Speculative design can still drive impact**

Even without a roadmap, Canvas helped shape conversations around what “collaboration” could mean in our product suite. It opened space for further exploration in *Partage*, *Classe*, and later features.

---

### **Internal advocacy**

I took the responsibility to present Canvas not just as an app concept, but as a *vision artifact*. With the head of product, I led presentations to our C-level execs, demonstrating animated flows, positioning slides, and mockups.

Some elements stuck. “Canvas mode” became a concept in later whiteboarding features. Teachers still ask for a shared screen where students can place notes. The conversation continues.

---

### **Role and collaboration**

I led the design strategy, prototyping, and concept articulation. But this was not a solo project.

* With our PO, we framed the PRD and gathered field feedback

* With the research lead, we analyzed use cases, behaviors, and adoption blockers

* With dev leads, we explored implementation feasibility and UI challenges on touchscreens

What made this project unique was the collaborative framing across disciplines — strategy, pedagogy, tech.

---

### **If I had to do it again**

Looking back, I would:

* Test earlier with simple interactive slideshows

* Pair more directly with teachers on session design

* Align sooner with strategic timelines of the broader SQOOL suite

---

### **Where it fits**

Canvas remains one of those projects that quietly influence the rest. Its DNA is present in the way *Partage* handles document timelines, or how *Classe* structures multi-step sessions.

It didn’t ship. But it shaped. And sometimes, in public service design, that’s impact too.

# **Case study 6000 words**

## **Canvas — Designing Real-Time Collaboration for Classrooms**

*A speculative journey into synchronous pedagogy, and why real-time tools need to meet teachers where they are.*

---

### **1\. It started in silence**

2021. Schools were starting to reopen after long months of digital scramble. In those odd hybrid weeks—half the students in class, the others behind a screen—one thing had become crystal clear: teaching had fundamentally changed.

At UNOWHY, we were coming off a massive push to rethink our ecosystem post-lockdown. As part of the product strategy team, I had worked on the early vision for “Gestion de classe,” a modern classroom control solution that bridged in-class tablet control with at-home visibility. But there was a bigger question looming.

**What does it mean to collaborate in education now?**

That question became a project we called Canvas.

It wasn’t a roadmap feature. It wasn’t scoped or committed. But it was urgent. In late 2021, we set aside six months to explore it. No delivery pressure. No feature deadlines. Just one big hypothesis:

*What if teachers and students could collaborate in real-time on the same surface—like in Figma, or Notion, or Miro—but designed for classrooms, pedagogy, and collective intelligence?*

Canvas was our speculative answer.

---

### **2\. First, we studied how teachers actually work**

Before drawing a single screen, we ran a discovery sprint. We partnered with pedagogy experts from classrooms across France. Primary teachers, tech-savvy math teachers, lycée philosophy teachers. What they all had in common? They were burned out.

Digital fatigue was real. Most of their remote experience had been pushing PDFs, managing chaotic Google Docs, and trying to follow up asynchronously.

We noticed something deeper:

Teachers weren’t **asking** for real-time collaboration.

What they wanted was **structure**. A way to prepare a session, deliver it cleanly, let students engage, and take back their work. One primary teacher told us:

“I don’t want to brainstorm with my 27 students. I want to guide them, give them tools, and help them reflect.”

We distilled this into our first constraint:

**Don’t replicate Figma or Miro. Design for pedagogy, not brainstorming.**

---

### **3\. Lo-fi prototyping to stretch the concept**

Armed with that insight, we built lo-fi prototypes. Paper first, then Framer and Principle. We tested four concepts with different teachers:

* **Board-locked:** Teacher controls the view; students can only annotate or comment.

* **Board-shared:** All collaborators can move, draw, and write.

* **Board-staged:** Teacher prepares the board in “scenes” that are revealed progressively.

* **Board-threaded:** Each activity (text, sticky, sketch) opens its own discussion thread.

The third won by a landslide. Teachers needed **asymmetry**. They wanted **control and flexibility**, not co-ownership. It became our key UX principle:

**The teacher’s board is a stage, not a jam session.**

We crafted a whiteboarding experience with a few essentials:

* Scene-based structure, with page transitions.

* A mode switcher: Prepare / Teach / Review.

* Activity zones: image drop, handwritten sketches, typed blocks, polls.

Every action was anchored in **instructional design**. We weren’t building tools. We were building lesson moments.

---

### **4\. UI concepts with deliberate simplicity**

In terms of visual identity, we avoided the default “edtech” look—no cartoonish icons, no pastel palettes.

Instead, we went minimal:

* **Neutral grays**, accented by one school-branded color

* A sidebar layout that resembled both Google Slides and OneNote

* Large touch targets (for tablets and interactive whiteboards)

* Thin yet legible typography (Geist, 14/20px ratio)

Teachers would toggle between “student view” and “teacher view” in the same interface. That constraint meant every element had to scale across cognitive load levels. We annotated each screen with pedagogical intent, not feature description.

For instance:

“This block lets students drag elements. But in Prep mode, the teacher defines zones and permissions.”

We validated that clarity with 5 teachers in live sessions.

---

### **5\. Why we stopped**

By April 2022, we had a fully specced PRD, mockups, animations, interaction specs, and a business case. We pitched Canvas internally at a strategy review. The reaction?

“It’s elegant, ambitious, but maybe too far from how classrooms actually work.”

They weren’t wrong. Teachers loved the *idea* of Canvas. But very few were ready to use it.

Reasons?

* Collaboration culture doesn’t exist in most classrooms.

* Infrastructure is inconsistent (whiteboards, tablets, old PCs).

* It was hard to slot Canvas into existing curricula.

And product-wise, we had more urgent battles to fight—deploying the core SQOOL suite, managing fleet infrastructures, delivering the MDM roadmap. So Canvas was paused. Not scrapped. But shelved.

---

### **6\. What stayed with us**

Canvas didn’t ship. But its DNA lives on.

* Its **asymmetrical interaction model** inspired parts of SQOOL Classe (ex: projecting content from teacher to student tablets).

* Its **visual system** shaped our design system refresh in 2023\.

* Its **user permission logic** informed the evolution of our cloud and sharing UX.

* And the **concept of modes** (Prep / Teach / Review) seeded the logic for the next generation of session design tools.

Internally, Canvas became a reference. A boundary-pushing exploration. Something we’d show new hires to say: “This is how far we’re willing to think.”

### **7\. Lessons I took as a designer**

Canvas taught me three things:

1. **Design is context-sensitive.** Tools like Figma or Miro thrive in product teams. In classrooms, the mindset is different. You can’t copy-paste tool paradigms.

2. **Designing for imagination is still designing.** Even if it doesn’t ship, a well-articulated concept creates shared language. It shows what’s possible.

3. **Sometimes the best work doesn’t go live.** And that’s okay. It shapes the culture. It influences the future.

Canvas was never a product. But it was a lens—a tool to stretch our thinking. A design that helped shape many others.
