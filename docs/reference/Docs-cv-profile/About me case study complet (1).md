About Me \+ design Philosophy (10.000 words)  
Part 1 – Foundations in Print and Creative Upbringing  
I didn’t learn design from a book. I learned it by watching my mother retouch magazine covers at 2am, placing images on flat plans with wax, and adjusting grids until the spacing between letters felt just right.  
My mother was a Senior Art Director and Editor-in-Chief for a nationally distributed French magazine. She taught me the foundations of design with precision and artistry: grid systems, photo manipulation, color grading, typography. She also happened to be an illustrator, caricaturist, and aquarelle painter. In the 90s, when I was still a teenager, she helped me discover Photoshop 6 on an old Macintosh. I’ve never stopped designing since.  
After high school, I studied graphic design at ISCOM, focusing on advertising and communication. During my internship at Publicis Et Nous, I worked as an assistant art director on luxury campaigns (Audemars-Piguet, Hermès, Printemps). I learned how to handle the constraints of visual storytelling across media — composition, concept, clarity — while working in fast-paced, high-demand environments.  
Between 2005 and 2009, I worked as an Art Director assistant in the magazine industry. I was responsible for magazine covers, photo selection, scanning, retouching, layout, and pre-print workflows. I collaborated closely with printers and managed the print chain through electronic flat plans. I still rely on these early foundations to bring precision and intentionality to all of my visual work.  
Outside of design, I’ve been a lifelong musician — first on guitar and bass, then in several bands (funk, hip-hop, downtempo). I produced beats and released music as a solo artist for years. I paused my musical activities in 2015 when my daughters were born, and I reconnected with rhythm in 2022 when I joined a Batucada percussion group as a snare drummer.  
Today, music and design remain deeply interconnected in the way I approach rhythm, structure, and flow in user interfaces.  
Part 2 \- From Print to Product  
Embracing UX, Systems Thinking and Collaborative Product Design  
The transition from print to digital didn’t happen overnight. But it felt like a natural shift. Around 2009, I started gravitating toward the web — not just as a container for content, but as a space where interaction happens. My visual skills gave me an advantage to craft polished interfaces, but I quickly realized that product design wasn’t about looks. It was about clarity. Feedback. Motion. Function. It was about making things that work.  
In the early 2010s, I was freelancing on small web and social platforms. I wore many hats — UI, UX, art direction, branding, dev handoff. But I wanted more structure. More long-term impact. That’s when I stepped fully into product design.  
From my early experiences, I carried with me three essential values:  
                     1\. Design happens in systems

 Whether it’s a print magazine grid or a component library in Figma, design has to be modular, coherent, and reusable. I don’t do decoration. I build systems.

                     2\. Design happens in teams

 I’ve never believed in the lone genius myth. Every good product I’ve worked on came from sharp discussions, shared ownership, and clear boundaries between discovery and delivery. I’m a designer who facilitates more than dictates.

                     3\. Design happens through friction

 The best ideas come from tension — between needs and constraints, vision and scope, elegance and feasibility. I’ve learned to embrace that friction, not fear it.

At Dailymotion, I worked on complex enterprise tools for media publishers. That’s when I started taking real ownership of flows, patterns, and documentation. My role was both visual and structural. I prototyped complex interactions, simplified publication workflows, and collaborated with PMs and engineers daily. I learned how to ship. I learned how to compromise without giving up precision.  
But it’s at UNOWHY that I entered a new chapter.  
Part 3 \- Designing at UNOWHY  
From Systems to Strategy (2018–2024)  
When I joined UNOWHY in 2018, the company was at a turning point. Known for its Android-based tablets and custom launcher aimed at middle schools, it was entering the era of educational platforms. But its ecosystem was fragmented — different interfaces for teachers, students, IT admins. Visual inconsistencies. No design system. And a growing product ambition that urgently needed structure.  
I came in as a Senior Product Designer, tasked initially with improving core apps. But quickly, the scope expanded.  
2018–2020 — Laying the Foundation  
I started by rebuilding the visual foundations:  
                        \* Reworking UI consistency across legacy apps (especially SQOOL Manager on desktop)

                        \* Introducing component thinking (building modular patterns rather than isolated screens)

                        \* Leading the redesign of SQOOL Séances, a web app to create and broadcast pedagogical sessions from teacher to student tablets

Around 2019, I initiated Hi-SQOOL, a prototype for a student-centric container app on tablets, bundling sessions, chat, file storage, and a clean onboarding. It was never fully developed, but it shaped the direction of the product vision: less launcher, more service platform.  
During this phase, I also started documenting patterns, building lightweight libraries, and mentoring junior designers.  
2020–2022 — Connect, La Bulle and the Shift to Web  
In 2020, with a new Head of Product, we launched a design sprint on a strategic topic: What comes after the aging Android launcher?  
I co-led the effort and designed Connect, a web-based dashboard for tablets and PCs:  
                           \* A modular home with shortcuts, usage analytics, quick actions

                           \* A searchable App Catalog designed like a storefront, with ratings and pedagogical tags

                           \* A new visual identity for dashboards: soft contrast, rounded modules, card hierarchy

Alongside Connect, I created a speculative concept called la Bulle — an animated, persistent UI element inspired by floating launchers. It lived at the edge of the screen, offering shortcuts, context-aware features (like screenshot and screen broadcast), and personality through animation.  
While the tech team didn’t implement it due to roadmap constraints, it became a vision artifact: something to inspire others and materialize a future user experience. I prototyped motion, designed specs, and pitched it at several internal presentations.  
Connect became a working demonstrator tested internally for over a year, influencing our direction.  
2022–2024 — From Vision to Suite  
By 2022, we pivoted toward a suite of independent web apps, each solving a clear need:  
                              \* SQOOL Classe: classroom supervision and device control

                              \* SQOOL Applications: curated app store for educators

                              \* SQOOL Partage: file sharing and cloud drive for the school ecosystem

                              \* SQOOL MDM: IT fleet and configuration manager

This shift required a new brand and design system. I took the lead on:  
                                 \* Collaborating with the agency Fllow for rebranding

                                 \* Designing app logos, icon systems, color codes, and typography for 5+ apps

                                 \* Building a Figma multi-library UI kit (button states, cards, dropdowns, dashboard layouts…)

                                 \* Structuring design operations for faster delivery across teams

I was also the primary design contact for all product squads. I shaped early concepts, helped translate strategy into design backlog, and briefed/mentored designers for handoff and delivery.  
Part 4 \- Strategic Leadership and Side Projects (2023–2024)  
Evolving My Role  
In 2023, I moved into a new position as Product Lead – UI & Interaction Design. My responsibilities expanded beyond individual projects or features. I became a strategic anchor for product and design inside UNOWHY — someone who could bridge concept and execution, vision and feasibility, design and engineering.  
This role change reflected a deep transformation in the company itself. From 60 employees in 2018, UNOWHY grew to over 250 by 2024\. The product portfolio expanded to 10+ apps. The design team matured. And we needed stronger cross-team consistency, faster iteration, clearer vision alignment.  
Leading with Strategy  
I started animating cross-functional workshops to help define product trajectories. With our Head of Product, I co-authored strategic decks for the CPO and CEO — framing long-term bets, positioning the software suite, connecting it with market opportunities.  
I contributed to:  
                                    \* The Road to 2024 and Road to 2025 product strategy presentations

                                    \* A renewed identity and visual language for the SQOOL suite

                                    \* Structured initiatives like the SQOOL Tribe “Ressources” (focused on transversal app management)

                                    \* Editorial content and changelogs to support feature launches

I became a referent in the company on interaction patterns, UI structure, design quality, and design storytelling. When we had to convince a stakeholder, brief an external team, or plan a pivot, I often crafted the visual and narrative backbone.  
Mentoring and Enabling Others  
I also stepped into a mentorship and enablement role. I was no longer designing every screen — I helped others deliver better design, faster:  
                                       \* Creating visual design kits, design ops rituals, and documentation

                                       \* Supervising complex UX problems (multi-target sharing flows, device control logic, etc.)

                                       \* Coaching junior designers and helping them grow into ownership

In 2023, I helped onboard a new front-end design lead and structured the collaboration between product, design, and tech teams.  
Outside the Product: Music and AI  
That same year, I reignited another part of my identity: music. After a long pause, I joined a Batucada percussion band, playing snare drum. It gave me energy, rhythm, and renewed clarity.  
At the same time, I launched Condamine Labs — my personal creative studio for experimenting with AI-powered apps. Using tools like Bolt, GPT-4, and custom design systems, I built over 30 app prototypes, each exploring a different idea or interaction model.  
Some are:  
                                          \* Creative tools (story generators, beatmakers, design prompts)

                                          \* Utility apps (AI form assistants, onboarding guides, file sorters)

                                          \* UX experiments (AI “scenarios” tools, generative UIs, LLM-based microflows)

This work shaped how I see the future of design: not just building static UIs, but crafting fluid, smart, responsive systems.  
A Shift in Practice  
In essence, 2023–2024 marked a paradigm shift in my practice:  
                                             \* From execution to system thinking

                                             \* From delivering screens to enabling a design culture

                                             \* From UI components to experience choreography

Part 5 \- Principles, Philosophy & What’s Next  
Designing from the Inside Out  
If there’s a common thread in my work, it’s this: I don’t design surfaces. I design from the inside out — starting from intent, behavior, and flow. Whether it’s a screen for a student on a tablet or an onboarding flow for a new app, my first question is:  
What’s the cognitive journey? What’s the interaction choreography?  
From there, I build upward: flows → patterns → visuals → animations.  
Always in context. Always trying to make the product feel understood, coherent, and natural.  
Pragmatic, Not Dogmatic  
I’m not a fan of dogmatic methods or frameworks. I’ve worked in startups, agencies, scale-ups, and public ecosystems — the only constant is change.  
My way of working is:  
                                                \* Strategic but hands-on: I prototype and ship.

                                                \* Systematic but flexible: I’ll build the design system and break it if the use case demands it.

                                                \* User-centered, but not just user-pleasing: I design for stakeholders, constraints, and sometimes even the politics.

Great design is pragmatic. It moves a product forward. It makes a team faster. It makes users feel smart.  
Principles That Guide Me  
A few core beliefs that shape how I design:  
                                                   \* Interaction should feel fluid, not mechanical. Tap, slide, focus, share — these aren’t just UI events. They’re gestures. And they deserve attention.

                                                   \* Interfaces must be generous. Good UIs don’t just respond. They anticipate. They reassure. They offer paths forward.

                                                   \* Clarity is a moral obligation. Especially in education and public service, a clear interface is a respectful interface. Confusing UIs create stress. Clarity empowers.

                                                   \* Design is a system of agreements. Between users and tools. Between product and brand. Between engineering and vision. My job is to align these agreements.

Influence and Inspiration  
I learned early on from print design: grids, color theory, contrast, typographic rhythm. I still draw from that lineage.  
I’m also deeply inspired by:  
                                                      \* Early Apple and Braun industrial design: precision, calmness

                                                      \* Motion and micro-interaction: how small changes can humanize software

                                                      \* Anthony Hobday’s framing of interaction principles: unspoken contracts, interface gravity, energy transfer

And I’ve always believed that the best designers are good storytellers — whether it’s to users or to a CEO.  
Giving Back  
As I gained experience, I found myself more often mentoring, coaching, or helping shape design orgs.  
I’ve helped junior designers grow, helped PMs define better user stories, helped executives understand how product and design interlock.  
One of the things I’m most proud of isn’t a mockup or an app. It’s seeing people in my team thrive, own their work, and get recognition.  
What’s Next  
I’m exploring new ways of working:  
                                                         \* More AI-assisted design: I’ve built 30+ apps with LLMs and want to go further into UX+AI convergence

                                                         \* More collaborative prototyping: design \+ product \+ code from day one

                                                         \* More public sharing: writing, speaking, making my design process transparent

I’m also curious about new contexts:  
                                                            \* Tools for learning

                                                            \* Interfaces for creative professionals

                                                            \* Civic tech and public platforms

But most of all, I want to keep building things that matter — with people who care.

About Me & Design Philosophy \- Complete Case 15.000 words  
Part 1 – Origins: A Childhood in Grids and Color (1,500–2,000 words)  
I didn't discover design through tutorials or bootcamps. I discovered it by watching my mother fix the alignment on a magazine spread at 2 a.m. in our living room, cigarette in hand, gridlines neatly etched in blue pencil. Before I understood what a grid system was, I understood that it made things feel calm.  
My mother was a Senior Art Director and Editor-in-Chief for a nationally distributed French lifestyle magazine. Her dual role made her a hybrid: visual thinker, editorial leader, typographer, image scout, layout architect. She was also an illustrator, a caricaturist, and an aquarelle painter. Her command of visual language wasn’t just professional; it was instinctive. She brought the same attention to a print ad as she did to a watercolor sketch of a market scene. I grew up inside this world—paper dummies on the table, Letraset type sheets, Pantone swatches, transparent rulers. I absorbed everything.  
In the mid-90s, we got a Macintosh at home. My mother installed Photoshop 6\. I was a teenager, and my evenings were divided between school homework and pixel experiments. I would take scans of magazine images and modify them—overexpose, blur, color grade, align text with obsessive precision. I didn’t know it then, but these nights laid the foundation for my future work in digital interface design.  
Learning by Doing: ISCOM and Early Professionalism  
After high school, I pursued graphic design and advertising studies at ISCOM. Unlike traditional design schools, ISCOM placed us closer to the pressure of communication: how a message travels, how visuals support business, how design supports a campaign objective. I appreciated the pragmatism.  
During my internship at Publicis Et Nous, I worked as an assistant art director on luxury campaigns. Clients included Printemps, Hermès, and Audemars-Piguet. It was a baptism by fire: late-night retouching, casting calls, building moodboards, designing storyboards for TV commercials. I learned what it meant to be pixel-perfect when a billboard was the canvas. I learned what elegance meant, how subtle the difference was between good and great. It sharpened my visual sensibility.  
2006–2009: Art Director at Déco Loisirs Magazine

Part 10 \- Design Philosophy: Systems, Rhythm & Clarity  
Designing from the Inside-Out  
In every project I’ve led, whether for a large-scale public platform or a micro-app built in a weekend, my design process begins at the core: behavior.  
I don’t start with color palettes. I don’t start with screens. I start by asking:  
What does the user need to do — and what does the interface need to understand to support that?  
From there, I design outward:  
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          \* From cognitive path → to interaction flow  
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          \* From user intent → to component response  
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          \* From system rules → to interface clarity  
This principle became especially clear while designing for educators at UNOWHY. Teachers, already managing the complexity of a live classroom, needed interfaces that made them feel in control — not overwhelmed. We weren’t designing just for efficiency. We were designing for reassurance.  
Pragmatic, Not Dogmatic  
Throughout my 15+ years in design, I’ve witnessed waves of methods and frameworks. From design thinking to dual-track agile, atomic design to jobs-to-be-done — each has value. But I believe in contextual pragmatism, not dogma.  
My working model is simple:  
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          \* Strategic, but hands-on — I prototype, I test, I adjust.  
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          \* Systematic, but flexible — I build design systems and break them when a use case demands it.  
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          \* Centered on users, but aware of politics — In public edtech or govtech, you design not just for users, but also for stakeholders, administrators, and public policies.  
Principles That Guide My Work  
1\. Interaction is a Choreography  
Click, swipe, pause, wait, retry. These are gestures, not just events. They create tempo, rhythm, expectation.  
I often draw from my background as a musician — understanding how tension and release, pacing and silence, all shape experience. The way a modal appears. The timing of a notification. The micro-delay in feedback. These are not aesthetic choices. They’re functional.  
2\. Interfaces Must Be Generous  
Generosity in UI is about:  
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          \* Reassuring the user  
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          \* Anticipating needs  
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          \* Providing escape hatches  
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          \* Offering subtle guidance without crowding the screen  
Whether for a dyslexic student or an overwhelmed IT admin, interfaces must feel like they care.  
3\. Clarity is an Act of Respect  
In education and public service, confusing interfaces create stress. Stress prevents learning. This is why clarity isn’t a nice-to-have. It’s a moral obligation.  
When we redesigned the Connect dashboard and built the SQOOL suite, I obsessed over:  
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          \* Navigational hierarchy  
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          \* Label semantics  
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          \* Microcopy that guides, not explains  
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          \* Layouts that breathe  
4\. Design Is a System of Agreements  
Design is not just visual. It’s a shared contract:  
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          \* Between the user and the tool  
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          \* Between product and brand  
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          \* Between design and engineering  
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          \* Between the platform and the public  
In complex orgs, this means maintaining alignment — not perfection. It means knowing when to fight for consistency, and when to flex.  
Inspirations and Tools  
Print Legacy  
I still carry with me the rules of grid, contrast, type rhythm, whitespace. From my print background, I learned:  
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          \* Don’t decorate — structure.  
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          \* Typography is architecture.  
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          \* Layout \= logic.  
Anthony Hobday’s Framing  
I’ve been deeply inspired by Hobday’s approach to interaction design:  
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          \* Unspoken contracts: What users expect but don’t verbalize.  
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          \* Interface gravity: Where the eye is pulled, what feels anchored.  
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          \* Energy transfer: How movement within an interface creates momentum.  
I often share these ideas in critiques and team reviews — not to impose rules, but to give language to what we sense.  
Team-first, Always  
Great design is never solo.  
I see my role as a facilitator, not a dictator. My strengths lie in:  
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          \* Clarifying strategy

Designing for public systems (like SQOOL or France VAE) means:

                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          \* Edge cases are real cases

In civic and public sector contexts, you don’t get the clean canvas of a startup. You get:  
                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  \* Layers of legacy systems

                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  \* Political constraints

                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  \* Accessibility requirements

                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  \* Deeply diverse users

                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  \* Long implementation timelines

And yet… the opportunity for impact is enormous. When you design a product that improves the daily life of a teacher, or helps a student access resources faster, or enables a school to better support inclusion — you’re not just shipping features. You’re shaping lives.  
That’s why I’ve chosen to stay in this space. Because design in public platforms is not about vanity. It’s about dignity. It’s where UI clarity becomes a public good. Where UX friction is not just a metric, but a moral cost.
